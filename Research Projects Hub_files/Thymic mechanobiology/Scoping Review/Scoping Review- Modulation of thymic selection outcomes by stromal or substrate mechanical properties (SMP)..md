#### 1.1 Thymocyte development and thymic selection.
The thymus is a primary lymphoid organ responsible for the generation of a functional and self-tolerant T-cell repertoire. During thymopoiesis, developing thymocytes migrate through anatomically distinct cortical and medullary microenvironments where they undergo positive selection, negative selection, agonist selection and lineage commitment into specialized T-cell populations, including CD4+ (T-helper cells), CD8+ (cytotoxic T-cells) and regulatory T (T<sub>reg</sub>) cells. As described in more detail in the document about T-cell development, TCPs travelling from the bone marrow arrive in the cortico-medullary junction through high endothelial venules (HEVs) and initially migrate towards the cortex and then after reaching the cortex start migrating back to the medullary regions. Once entering the thymus, the TCPs begin expressing CD7 and are now referred to be in pro-T1 stage or DN-1 (double negative, CD4- CD8-). Then as they progress through their developmental processes in the thymus, they undergo a pathway which can be summarized as: 
pro-T1/DN-1 → pro-T2/DN-2 (CD7+ CD5+) → DN-3 (β-selection occurs) → DN-4 → DP (double positive, CD4+ CD8+) → **positive selection** → CD4/CD8 lineage choice → **negative selection/T<sub>reg </sub>development** → mature SP (single positive, CD4+ or CD8+) T-cell. 
An important thing to keep in mind is that this is not a perfectly linear sequence, with spatial and temporal overlaps often being observed between positive and negative selection. The DP stage is where the selection process starts to dominate the fate of the thymocytes as they now express a complete TCRαβ–CD3 complex. TCRα rearrangement generates enormous receptor diversity, after which each thymocyte effectively undergoes a functional test during the selection process against self-pMHC displayed by thymic antigen-presenting cells (APCs). The cortex is the primary site for positive selection with APCs such as cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTECs) doing the main job of expressing the pMHC-I/II complexes to the DP thymocytes. A thymocyte whose TCR cannot productively interact with either MHC class is not rescued and dies by neglect. Additionally, while there are still some unanswered questions regarding the lineage choice between CD4+ and CD8+, the main factor is considered to be the type of MHC complex a DP thymocyte interacts with the most (with MHC-I promoting CD8+ lineage and MHC-II promoting CD4+ lineage). Following lineage decision, SP thymocytes begin to undergo the negative selection process, occurring primarily in the medullary region. This spatial limitation seems to be mainly due to the role of medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs), which have the unique capacity to express tissue-restricted antigens, with the transcriptional regulator AIRE (Autoimmune regulator) being particularly important in this process. These antigens can subsequently be presented to developing thymocytes, allowing potentially autoreactive clones to be deleted (referred to as negative selection or clonal deletion). There are numerous factors which are involved in deciding whether a thymocyte is deleted during these processes, including TCR signal duration, ligand affinity/kinetics, co-receptor participation, developmental stage and APC context. However, it has been observed that not every thymocyte receiving relatively strong self-reactive signals is deleted, with a subset differentiating into Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells (T<sub>reg</sub>). The precise relationship between TCR signal strength, cytokines, co-stimulation and T<sub>reg</sub> fate is complex and provides another example of why "signal strength" cannot simply be equated with a binary survival/death decision. 

#### 1.2 Mechanosensing capabilities of TCR and role of co-receptors.
The conventional αβ-TCR consists of an αβ heterodimer associated with the CD3 signaling subunits, principally CD3γε, CD3δε and CD3ζζ-containing components. The αβ chains provide most of the antigen-recognition interface, whereas CD3 contains the intracellular ITAMs (immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs) that initiate signaling after receptor engagement. The typical signaling pathway is described as TCR–pMHC binding → mechanical/structural perturbation → Lck/ZAP-70 activation → ITAM phosphorylation → LAT/SLP-76 signalosome → Ca²⁺, Ras/MAPK, cytoskeletal and transcriptional responses.
One of the main aspects of TCR signal activation has emerged to be sensitivity to mechanical forces, with force-dependent catch-bond behavior and cytoskeletal tension playing important roles in ligand discrimination. The catch bond behavior, also observed in adhesion receptors like selectins and integrins, is rather intriguing because it results in the lifetime of a bond increasing when mechanical force is applied to it (upto a specific threshold) contrasting with slip-bond behavior in which lifetime decreases with increasing applied force. Structural studies have showed that TCR contains mechanically responsive regions in its variable and constant domains, including the Cβ FG-loop. The CD3 extracellular domains form relatively rigid structures, while the connections between extracellular and transmembrane regions can transmit mechanical perturbations into the membrane-spanning receptor complex. Specific structural features, including oxidized CxxC motifs in CD3 components, have been proposed to facilitate force transmission through the complex, with mechanisms such as changes domain tilting, hinge rotation and extension of flexible regions, being explored to get in-depth understanding of TCR mechanotransductional capabilities. 

In addition to the main TCR receptor, various co-receptors like CD4, CD8 and CD28 also play important roles in binding to MHCs or other surface molecules on APCs. Their cytoplasmic tails of CD4 and CD8 (which bind to MHC-II and MHC-I, respectively) associate with the Src-family kinase Lck, bringing it in close proximity with the TCR–CD3 complex, allow the downstream pathways to activate more easily. CD28 operates in a different manner to CD4/8 as it binds to CD80 (B7-1) and CD86 (B7-2) on APCs and its engagement enhances signaling generated by TCR ligation by recruiting proteins including PI3K, GRB2 and other signaling components. and this has been seen to promote proliferation, survival, metabolism and cytokine production.

#### 1.3 Mechanobiology of the thymus 
Historically, research on thymocyte development and selection has focused predominantly on biochemical mechanisms, including TCR signaling, cytokines, chemokines, Notch signaling and antigen presentation. In contrast, comparatively little attention has been paid to the potential contribution of mechanical factors within the thymic microenvironment. However, this trend is now changing with recent advances in the fields of mechanobiology demonstrating that mechanical cues (including substrate stiffness, viscoelasticity, porosity and extracellular matrix/ECM organization) can profoundly regulate immune cell behavior. Mechanical properties of the cellular microenvironment influence cytoskeletal organization, mechanotransduction pathways, cell migration and receptor signaling across numerous biological systems. This area of research is still quite underexplored which can be seen by the fact that currently a consensus has not been established regarding the value ranges of important mechanical properties of the thymus such bulk stiffness and comparisons between regions regarding their stiffness. But some work has been done to explore these areas with the researchers rely on high-resolution biophysical techniques to differentiate the mechanical environments of the cortex and the medulla due to the highly heterogenous nature of thymic tissue. Two of the methodologies that have been used to measure thymic stiffness are:
- **Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) Nanoindentation**: This is the primary method used to capture regional differences. By applying a microscopic cantilever probe to living or native thymic tissue slices, researchers can generate high-resolution "force maps" or surface stiffness topographies. 
- **Shear Wave Elastography (SWE)**: On a macro scale, clinical ultrasound-based SWE is used to track overall thymic stiffness in humans (revealing a baseline healthy range of 6.76 ± 1.04 kPa, though its spatial resolution is usually too low to separate individual cortical and medullary boundaries in vivo.
Some important findings from these studies include: 
- **Native Tissue Modulus**: Studies utilizing AFM force mapping on native, cell-dense thymus tissue slices reported a mean Young's modulus (denoted by 'E' and is a measure of stiffness) averaging around 12 ± 6 kPa.
- **The Cortical vs. Medullary Pattern**: Micro-scale force mapping based experiments revealed a distinct recurring distribution pattern. The cortex registers on the higher end of the stiffness spectrum (closer to 15–18 kPa) due to tight packing of thymocytes, while the medulla sits on the lower, more compliant end. 
- **Cellular vs. Extracellular Contributions**: Interestingly, when researchers decellularize the thymus to measure only the structural ECM scaffold, the overall stiffness profile shifted, with the E peaking around 5.5 kPa and loses its stark regional variance. This indicates that the biological stiffness gradient experienced by a developing thymocyte is overwhelmingly driven by living cell-packing density rather than just the background matrix.

Another critical source of interest in thymic mechanobiology is the process of thymic involution, which is accompanied by extensive structural remodeling of the stromal compartment appearing as fibrosis, adipose replacement and changes in extracellular matrix composition. Such alterations are expected to modify the mechanical properties of the tissue, yet their implications for thymocyte development remain poorly understood. This, combined with an increasing number of studies demonstrating the importance of mechanical forces and catch-bond behavior, provides a push towards the pursuit of direct evidence about the role of mechanical properties of thymic tissue and mechanical forces generated during TCR–pMHC interactions/acting upon this bond in influencing thymocyte development (especially the selection process). This push is necessary as despite growing work across immunology, mechanobiology and tissue engineering, the literature on these questions remains fragmented. Relevant studies span multiple disciplines and experimental systems, ranging from native thymic tissue to engineered hydrogels and synthetic biomaterials. Consequently, no comprehensive synthesis currently exists that maps how thymic stromal mechanical properties have been investigated in relation to thymocyte selection and associated biological processes.

Therefore, a scoping review is appropriate to systematically identify, classify and summarize the available evidence while highlighting important gaps for future experimental and computational research.     


### Section 2: Objectives
This scoping review was conducted in order to systematically map the research done in the area of T-cell selection mechanobiology, as well as to identify any existing gaps in knowledge. For this review, the following research question was formulated: "What evidence exists linking thymic SMPs (such as stiffness, viscoelasticity or porosity) to mechanisms governing thymocyte selection, central tolerance and involution?" Due to the interdisciplinary nature of this primary research question, the following evidence modules were devised to cover different aspect of relevant data in the literature: 
- **EM-1**: To assess evidence linking SMPs with positive selection, negative selection, T<sub>reg </sub>production and other relevant aspects of thymocyte development (for eg., formation of DP thymocytes);
- **EM-2**: To identify mechanotransduction pathways implicated in the selection process or other relevant aspects of thymocyte development (pathways arising solely from receptor-ligand interactions between adjacent cells, e.g., force-dependent Notch activation, will be excluded unless linked to SMPs or ECM remodeling);
- **EM-3**: To examine how SMPs influences TCR signaling and thymocyte properties that can significantly modulate TCR signaling (including migration/encounter rate with APCs, membrane stiffness/spreading, cytoskeletal organization and intracellular calcium levels);
- **EM-4**: To summarize evidence on changes in thymic selection process and SMPs during aging and disease.
Each included study was assigned to one or more evidence modules based on its primary outcome measure(s) or topic(s) of discussion. Studies reporting multiple outcomes (e.g., both stiffness and migration) were coded to multiple modules but were counted once in the total study characteristics.


### Section 3: Methodology for the single-author scoping review

#### 3.1 Study design

This review was conducted as a scoping review following the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O'Malley and further refined by Levac et al., and was reported according to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).

Eligibility criteria for the final data synthesis: 
	Papers that were included:
	- investigate thymic stromal biology, thymic architecture, mechanotransduction or ECM mechanobiology;
	- examine positive selection, negative selection, central tolerance, thymic involution, or related developmental processes;
	- study how mechanical properties of the microenvironment or mechanical forces applied by intracellular machinery modulate TCR signaling or T-cell activation.
	- are available in English;
	- were published following 01/01/2010.
	Papers that were excluded:
	- report clinical outcomes of thymectomy or thymic disease without mechanistic investigation;
	- focus entirely on TCR mechanobiological questions (such as what conformational changes happen in TCR regions which lead to signal transmission or catch bond formation following force application and how much force is applied by the TCR on pMHC or vice versa on synthetic surfaces or in solution) without reference to any SMPs.
	- lack relevance to thymic selection, mechanotransduction, or stromal biology;
	- focus on engineered thymic tissues solely for regenerative or transplantation purposes without addressing mechanobiological mechanisms;
	- are conference abstracts, editorials, or non-peer-reviewed opinion pieces.

#### 4.2 Influence of SMPs upon thymocyte selection and other developmental processes.
The evidence hierarchy for this section is as follows:
- **Direct Evidence:** Studies which show how SMPs influence positive/negative/agonist selection and development of central tolerance;
- **Indirect evidence:** Studies which show SMPs influence the lineage decisions in mature T-cells.

Emerging evidence in the literature points towards a link between substrate mechanical properties and thymocyte selection and other lineage decisions, with 6 studies providing relevant data for better understanding this connection. As it can be read in the [[(EM-1) Info regarding the link between substrate mechanical properties (SMPs) and thymocyte selection or other aspects of development.]] document, current evidence encompasses both thymus-specific experimental systems and studies of mature or differentiating T cells exposed to defined mechanical environments, however many important questions and mechanistic details still remain unanswered. 

The data reported in the study by N. Jeffreys et. al. (2025) is the only study that can be classified into the direct evidence category as it uses a thymic mimetic environment and studies how thymocyte differentiation is directly modulated by SMPs. However, the study examines progenitor T-cell differentiation rather than canonical thymocyte selection events such as positive or negative selection. The authors developed 3D bio-engineered niches (referred to as engineered thymic niches or ETNs, described briefly in section 4.1) to study the role of mechanical cues in differentiation of CD34+ human HSCs towards either the lymphoid or myeloid lineage. One of their significant findings was that softer and more viscous matrices enhanced the emergence and differentiation velocity of CD7+CD5+ progenitor T cells (which are considered as pro-T2 cells that have entered the thymus and are also called DN-2, as described in more detail in section 1), whereas stiffer and more elastic matrices substantially impaired T-lineage differentiation. The effect was evident both in the overall number and frequency of CD7+CD5+ cells and in levels of CD7 expression. Conversely, CD34+ cells exposed to the stiffer, more elastic matrices displayed a relative bias toward CD33+ myeloid-competent progenitors despite the presence of exogenous thymopoietic cues. Thus, matrix mechanics were sufficient to influence the balance between lymphoid/T-lineage and myeloid developmental trajectories within a thymus-mimetic system. These mechanical effects were accompanied by changes in the transcriptional program associated with T-cell development. Following 96 hours in soft-viscous engineered thymic niches, cells exhibited increased expression of Notch1 pathway-associated genes, including TCF7, Hes1, Deltex and Notch1, together with reduced expression of the myeloid-associated transcription factor PU.1 and the HSC self-renewal gene E2A. These findings suggest that ECM mechanical properties may influence lineage commitment by altering the activity or maintenance of developmental gene regulatory networks. Importantly, this study provides evidence linking the physical properties of a thymus-mimetic ECM directly to human progenitor T-cell differentiation rather than merely demonstrating an effect of substrate mechanics on mature T-cell function. However, a point to keep in mind is that this study is a pre-print which hasn't undergone peer-review and certain interpretations of reported data might change in the future.

Mechanical forces have also been directly implicated in thymocyte selection. Hong et. al. (2018), using in-vitro assays such as biomembrane force probe (BFP), demonstrated that the magnitude and persistence of forces generated by thymocytes through the TCR and CD8 coreceptor distinguish ligands associated with positive versus negative selection. Negative-selection ligands induced more sustained forces and supported cooperative trimolecular TCR–pMHC–CD8 catch bonds, whereas positive-selection ligands generated weaker and less sustained forces and were associated with shorter-lived bimolecular slip bonds (either TCR-pMHC or CD8-pMHC). Disruption of actin polymerization with latrunculin A or inhibition of myosin contractility with Y-27632 reduced the mechanical interaction between thymocytes and pMHC, demonstrating that the forces were generated through the thymocyte actomyosin machinery. These findings establish mechanical force as a functional determinant of the selection response. However, because the study primarily examines forces generated internally by thymocytes in response to TCR engagement and does not directly manipulate the mechanical properties of thymic stroma or ECM, it represents evidence for mechanoregulation of selection rather than direct evidence that the stromal mechanical environment controls selection. It is therefore relevant to EM-1 but should be distinguished from ECM-dependent mechanisms.

Another study which employs a thymic mimetic environment is by J.W. Hickey et. al. (2019). They used alginate hydrogels-based 3D matrices (artificial T-cell stimulating matrices or aTMs, described in section 4.1) to demonstrate that substrate stiffness can alter the phenotype of expanding human CD8+ T-cells independently of overall expansion. They reported that stiffer aTMs generated a more balanced ratio of central memory (which reside in lymph nodes) to effector memory (which patrol the peripheral tissues) CD8+ T-cells than the softer aTMs (with the effector memory cells dominating at 0.5 kPa). Although this data shows a direct link between SMPs and lineage decisions, it is categorized into the indirect evidence category as it focusses on mature CD8+ cells and not on thymocytes. Other primary studies put into the indirect evidence category reinforce the effect of mechanical environments on mature T-cell proliferation and phenotype. Pandit et al. (2025) found that exposure to stiff, patterned or non-patterned surfaces accelerated proliferation of central and effector memory T-cell populations relative to naïve T cells, although differences between mechanical environments diminished at later stages of culture. Conversely, O'Connor et. al. (2012) reported greater expansion of human naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells on softer PDMS substrates, with ~ fourfold greater overall expansion on the softest compared with the stiffest substrates. Softer substrates also favored retention of a CD62L-high, memory-like phenotype, whereas stiffer substrates were associated with a relative shift toward CD62L-negative cells (that are generally classified as effector memory T-cells). These apparently divergent findings highlight that the effect of mechanical stiffness is context-dependent and may depend on substrate chemistry, ligand presentation, stiffness range, T-cell differentiation state and duration of mechanical exposure. 

The review articles included in this evidence module provide further evidence from peripheral T-cell systems further supports the broader capacity of mechanical environments to regulate T-cell developmental state and function. Substrate stiffness has been shown to influence regulatory T-cell (T<sub>reg</sub>) induction, although the direction and magnitude of the response depend on the mechanical regime and experimental context. In mouse CD4+ T cells, relatively soft substrates promoted T<sub>reg </sub>differentiation, whereas increasing stiffness promoted T<sub>reg</sub> induction in human cells over a different stiffness range, with the latter response associated with increased mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Nataraj. et al., 2018; Shi et. al., 2023). Stiffness can additionally influence T<sub>reg</sub> suppressive function, persistence and epigenetic stability, indicating that mechanical cues can affect not only lineage-associated phenotype but also functional maturation. These findings cannot be directly extrapolated to thymic selection, but demonstrate that T-cell differentiation is sensitive to the mechanical properties of the extracellular environment.

Taken together, the available evidence supports the broader conclusion that mechanical properties can influence T-lineage developmental outcomes, but the strength of evidence differs substantially across biological contexts. The strongest evidence directly relevant to EM-1 comes from thymus-mimetic ECM systems, in which matrix viscoelasticity alters human progenitor T-cell differentiation and lineage bias (Jeffreys et. al., 2025). Hong et. al. (2018) provides complementary evidence that mechanically generated forces directly influence thymocyte positive/negative selection, although the source of the mechanical signal is primarily the thymocyte rather than the stromal microenvironment. Studies using mature or differentiating peripheral T-cells consistently demonstrate that mechanical conditions can modify proliferation, differentiation, phenotype and functional state, but these findings should be regarded as indirect evidence for thymic biology. 


#### 4.3 Mechanotransduction pathways in thymocyte selection and other developmental functions
The evidence hierarchy for this section is as follows:
- **Direct Evidence:** Studies which report the role of specific mechanotransduction pathways in connecting SMPs to thymocyte selection.
- **Indirect evidence:** Studies which report about these pathways and SMPs in mature T-cells.

Mechanotransductional pathways have been known to play crucial roles in influencing stem cell differentiation in different contexts, from hematopoietic niches to muscle regeneration following micro-damage. Consequently, it is not of great surprise that these pathways are now being explored in the context of thymic selection and other related lineage decisions, with 4 out of 20 studies in the final data extraction providing a potential link between SMPs and cellular signaling, rather than representing force-dependent receptor–ligand interactions between adjacent cells in isolation. As documented in [[(EM-2) Info on mechanotransductional pathways involved in T-cell selection process and other aspects of thymocyte development.]], ECM mechanics, integrin-mediated adhesion, actomyosin contractility, mechanosensitive ion channels, and force transmission to the nucleus represent potential mechanotransduction pathways through which stromal biomechanical properties may influence thymopoiesis.  

Similar to EM-1 data, the most direct evidence is provided by N. Jeffreys et. al. (2025). This study reports the interaction between substrate mechanics, integrin adhesion and Notch1 signaling during human progenitor T-cell differentiation. Notch1–DLL4 signaling is essential for T-lineage commitment, while integrin-mediated adhesion to VCAM-1 and collagen-I has been reported to synergize with Notch1 signaling to enhance the velocity of human progenitor T-cell differentiation. While Notch signaling is generally associated to cell-cell interactions (which is beyond the scope of this study), the authors connected it to the ECM's mechanics by binding DLL4 to scaffold fibers using click chemistry. Integrin engagement provides a physical connection between the extracellular environment and the actin cytoskeleton, potentially allowing matrix mechanical properties to regulate the tensile forces experienced by Notch1–DLL4 complexes. In this model, substrate mechanics could therefore modulate Notch1 activation indirectly through integrin–cytoskeletal mechanotransduction. The role of integrins in influencing stemness of T-cells in engineered systems has also been discussed by Zubia-Aranburu et.al. (2026), who reported enhanced proliferation whilst preserving stemness of CAR T-cells in 3D fibrin matrices. This response was attributed to integrin-β2-dependent mechanotransduction, with matrix stiffness associated with YAP-inactivation and preservation of stemness-associated gene expression while MAPK-dependent proliferation was maintained. Although CAR T cells are not thymocytes, these findings provide evidence that integrin-dependent sensing of 3D matrix mechanics can regulate the balance between proliferation and differentiation in T-lineage cells. They therefore support the plausibility of analogous ECM–integrin–cytoskeletal pathways operating in mechanically regulated thymocyte development, while remaining indirect evidence for the thymic selection process.

An intriguing point is that matrix viscosity was given a particular importance amongst other mechanical properties by N. Jeffreys et.al. As the data showed that softer, more viscous matrices allowed greater and faster differentiation of CD34+ HSCs towards lymphoid lineage, the authors hypothesize that these matrices may permit formation and mechanical loading of Notch1–DLL4 and integrin–substrate bonds while allowing cellular contractility to dissipate or redistribute mechanical energy. In contrast, stiffer or more purely elastic substrates may favor load-induced bond dissociation and alter focal adhesion dynamics. This provides a potential mechanism by which the mechanical state of the thymic ECM could influence the duration and magnitude of Notch1 signaling and, consequently, the rate of progenitor T-cell differentiation. At present, however, aspects of this mechanism remain hypothetical and require direct experimental validation, particularly with respect to the relationship between defined matrix viscoelasticity, force transmission through integrins, Notch1 cleavage and downstream thymocyte differentiation. Another probable mechanism  proposed by the authors involved the functioning of actomyosin machinery and linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC), which provide a mechanical route from the cell cortex to the nucleus. They propose that contractility-mediated nuclear deformation could alter nuclear pore complex (NPC) behavior and thereby influence nucleocytoplasmic transport of NICD and other regulatory factors involved in the T-cell developmental gene regulatory network. While this mechanism has well established evidence in other cell models (like mesenchymal stem cells and metastatic cells), the evidence which directly demonstrates such a pathway during human thymopoiesis remains limited, and it should therefore be considered a mechanistic hypothesis rather than an established pathway. This idea of nuclear mechanics being the link between SMPs and T-cell responses is also discussed by Saitakis et. al. (2017). This group proposed that differences in T-cell responses to substrate stiffness could involve transmission of mechanical signals to the nucleus through the nuclear lamina, thereby altering gene expression. Like the previously mentioned role of actomyosin machinery and LINC in influencing thymocyte development, the role of lamins and nuclear mechanics in mediating cellular responses to matrix stiffness has been reported in other cell systems (like stem cells) but direct evidence linking lamin-dependent mechanotransduction to thymocyte selection remains lacking. 

In addition to integrins, Notch signaling, YAP functioning, actomyosin networks and LINC, one more important aspect of mechanotransductional pathways is the functioning of mechanosensitive ion channels, which provide another potential route through which SMPs may influence thymic selection and lineage decisions. Piezo1 is particularly relevant as it activity can couple membrane deformations to intracellular calcium-dependent signaling (which plays a central role in TCR signaling pathway). Evidence summarized by Wu & Ye (2025) indicates that Piezo1 regulates T-cell differentiation and that its deletion in CD4+ T cells alters TGF-β1 signaling, regulatory T-cell expansion, and Th1/Th17 polarization. Mechanical stiffness can also regulate cytokine secretion by dendritic cells through Piezo1, indirectly influencing T-cell differentiation. Zubia-Aranburu et. al. (2019) also discussed the negative role of Piezo1 in T-cell functioning by upregulating GRHL3 (a transcription factor), which in turn induces the expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF114. RNF114 then binds to F-actin, promoting its downregulation and reorganization which was then observed to decrease the traction forces at the immunological synapse. However, whether Piezo1 directly regulates thymocyte selection or developmental transitions within the thymus has not yet been established.

Overall, the evidence identifies several interconnected candidate mechanotransduction pathways relevant to EM-2: ECM/integrin adhesion, actomyosin contractility, force-dependent modulation of Notch1 signaling, modulation of gene expression by YAP, Piezo1-mediated mechanosensing and transmission of mechanical forces to the nucleus through the LINC complex and nuclear lamina. Of these, the strongest direct mechanistic link to thymocyte development currently involves the coupling of matrix mechanics to integrin adhesion, actomyosin contractility and Notch1-dependent progenitor T-cell differentiation (Jeffreys et. al., 2025). Data from other studies is comparatively indirect, deriving largely from peripheral T cells, CAR T-cell systems or other mechanobiological models. Importantly, the available literature does not yet establish a definitive mechanotransduction pathway by which stromal or ECM mechanics regulate thymocyte selection. Rather, it identifies a set of plausible mechanical signaling axes that warrant investigation, particularly at the interface between ECM composition and mechanics, integrin engagement, cytoskeletal tension, and TCR/Notch-dependent developmental signaling.



#### 4.4 Modulation of TCR signaling and associated processes by SMPs
The evidence hierarchy for this section is as follows:
- **Direct Evidence:** Studies which demonstrate how SMPs directly affect TCR signaling intensity in thymocytes;
- **Indirect evidence:** Studies which demonstrate how SMPs directly affect TCR signaling intensity in mature T-cells through other associated processes such as migration/encounter rate with APCs, membrane stiffness/spreading, cytoskeletal organization and intracellular calcium levels;
- **Mechanistic supporting evidence**: Studies that did not directly manipulate stromal or substrate mechanical properties but investigated intracellular mechanotransduction components (e.g., actin, myosin II, talin, vinculin) were included only where these components represent established downstream mediators of cellular responses to mechanical stimuli. Such studies were treated as indirect mechanistic evidence rather than direct evidence of stromal mechanoregulation as they suggest a plausible mechanistic chain linking stromal mechanical properties to TCR signaling through cytoskeletal organization. 

Substrate mechanical properties (SMPs) can influence T-cell responses by altering the physical conditions under which TCR ligands are encountered, engaged and mechanically coupled to the T-cell cytoskeleton. In this search, no study was obtained that directly explored the relationship between SMPs and TCR signaling intensity in thymocytes and only a few studies were found directly explored the link between SMPs and TCR signaling or T-cell activation. However, numerous studies amongst the final set (16 out of 20, [[(EM-3) Info about different aspects of TCR functioning that are or can be influenced by substrate mechanics.]]) mentioned the significance of the cytoskeletal networks (primarily, the actin network) in modulating TCR signaling. The decision to take the role of cytoskeletal network in modulating TCR signaling and associated processes as indirect evidence for the influence of SMPs is based two points. The first is the presence of a well established in literature regarding the intimate relationship between SMPs and the state of cytoskeletal networks. And the second is the observation that the direct connection (between SMPs and thymocyte TCR signaling) has not been explored sufficiently so discussing the associated evidence can be beneficial in providing directions for future work to address this research gap. However, it must be clarified that this indirect evidence is restricted to only the events in the which the control flows from cytoskeleton to TCR signaling (for eg., fusion of membrane TCR microclusters and application of force on the TCR-pMHC to induce catch bond dynamics) and not the opposite such as induction of actin polymerization and flow in immunological synapses following TCR-ligand binding. Taken together, these findings suggest a plausible mechanistic chain linking stromal mechanical properties to TCR signaling through cytoskeletal organization, while highlighting the relative lack of studies directly testing this relationship in thymic systems.

This distinction between direct and indirect regulation is important for interpreting the relevance of these findings to thymocytes. A substrate may influence TCR signaling directly when the TCR ligand is mechanically coupled to the substrate and the thymocyte/T-cell generates forces against it. Alternatively, the substrate may modify cytoskeletal organization, cell shape, migration or membrane dynamics, thereby changing the probability and characteristics of TCR engagement without directly altering the biochemical properties of the receptor–ligand interaction.

##### 4.4.1 SMPs → TCR signaling intensity and relevant downstream effects
The clearest demonstrations that SMPs can directly influence TCR signaling are present in certain studies which mechanically couple TCR ligands to substrates of defined stiffness including Hui et. al. (2014), M. Saitakis et.al. (2017), A. Wahl et. al. (2018), J.W. Hickey et. al (2019) and J.J. Pandit et. al. (2025). A review article by Hivroz and Saitakis (2017) extensively discussed relevant experiments that showed that mouse naïve CD4+ T-cells activated through anti-CD3ϵ displayed greater IL-2 production and increased early phosphorylation of ZAP-70 and Src-family kinases on PA substrates of 100-200 kPa stiffness compared to softer ones. Importantly, this stiffness-dependent response was abolished by inhibition of myosin II through the use of blebbistatin, indicating that substrate mechanics were interpreted through a force-generating cytoskeletal system rather than acting independently of cellular contractility. Similar observations have been reported by M. Saitakis et.al. in human lymphoblastoid effector T-cells (mature cells have been activated and driven into a large, rapidly dividing blast-like or lymphoblastoid state), in which TCR stimulation on increasingly rigid PDMS substrates (~2 MPa) produced greater tyrosine phosphorylation than softer substrates (~5 kPa) and this increase was more pronounced when ICAM-1 was also present, pointing towards a synergistic effect of LFA1-ICAM1 binding. Stiffer substrates resulting in higher levels of phospho-tyrosine (pY) compared to softer substrates was also reported by Hui et.al., however, the pY levels persisted for longer in softer substrates which might points towards the functioning of a negative feedback-loop that keeps the TCR signaling from getting too activated.  

Multiple other studies have explored the role of LFA1-ICAM1 in TCR-pMHC bond formation and further downstream signaling in T-cells, but as LFA1-ICAM1 binding primarily represents the interactions with T-cells and APCs instead of T-cell interaction with the ECM, it is not discussed in depth in this review unless their binding is connected to SMPs (like in the study mentioned above). However, the role of other integrins has also been discussed in some of the other studies, such as VLA-4 whose ligand includes fibronectin (results about this will be discussed in an upcoming subsection). F. S. Majedi et. al. (2020) demonstrated that the presence of RGD peptide (which bind to multiple integrins and is found in fibronectin, vitronectin, collagen, osteopontin, fibrinogen, and bone sialoprotein) resulted in T-cells becoming more activated than when in samples where this peptide motif was absent. The T-cells in an environment lacking RGD were even less activated than those cultured in soft 2D or 3D environments with RGD, despite the ample presence of stimulatory beads. This points to towards an important role of integrins--providing tethering for T-cells to exert force on TCR-pMHC. Another explanation regarding the importance of integrin binding to ECM or APCs in TCR signaling is provided by Beemiller & Krummel (2010) who explain that beyond the adhesion strength supplied to hold the T-APC couple together, integrin binding drives the shear induced bond rupture by facilitating dynamic actin cytoskeletal polymerization that will generate the shear force needed to test recently bound TCRs.  

As studies generally use relatively homogenous substrates to explore T-cell mechanosensing, J.J. Pandit et.al. aimed to move one step further in the exploration of T-cell mechanosensing. This was done by asking how would T-cells respond to substrates with more heterogenous mechanical environments which would be a slightly more faithful representation of physiological environments of T-cells. They pursued the answer to this question using mechanically biphasic surfaces patterned with alternating soft and stiff microdomains (mentioned in section 4.1). Their conclusion was that T-cells do not average the mechanical signals produced by the microdomains with varying stiffness but rather produce relatively low activation equal to that of the homogeneous soft surface. This conclusion is quite relevant for studies which aim to replicate thymic or lymphoid tissues for thymocyte/T-cell culturing. One more intriguing aspect of the results published by this group was regarding the relationship between activation time and sensitivity to substrate mechanics. They showed that activation of T-cells for long stimulation time (three days) produced very high CD25 expression (also known as IL-2Ra, which is considered an activation marker), but the differences among the surfaces were mostly non-significant, and inconsistent across samples from different donors. This finding is supported by the data reported by M. Saitakis et. al., according to which in shorter time frames, stiffness modulates TCR-induced activation only in extreme conditions (100 kPa). However, at later time points (48 hr, 72 hr), metabolic remodeling, cell cycle progression and proliferation were modified for the whole range of stiffness tested (500 Pa to 100 kPa) suggesting that response to stiffness builds on with time. Such latency may be related to amplification loops induced by cytokines, which require time to be produced. 

Another unique method of exploring T-cell mechanosensing in more faithful representations of physiological environments was reported by C. Trenzinger et. al. (2025). Instead of using 2D cultures or 3D scaffolds to control a cell's mechanical environment, they fabricated a microfluidics-based device to apply controlled mechanical forces on the T-cells by creating “mechanical sandwich” in which T cells are placed between two functionalized glass surfaces (actuator and carrier), and one of the surfaces (the actuator) can be pushed down to precisely compress/confine the cells. Additionally, the authors use spacer beads to determine the final distance between the two surfaces and to create a self-leveling mechanism (i.e. when the actuator is pushed against the carrier, the rigid glass disc can settle against the spacer beads and establish a relatively uniform gap). Intriguing data came from this study showing confinement led to an increase of pErk1/2 signal and Ca+2 levels in the resting state, but in the activating state, ==a slight decrease in these two signals was observed.== 

##### 4.4.2 SMPs → Cell spreading, membrane deformation and TCR organization
Substrate mechanics may regulate TCR signaling partly by altering cell spreading, membrane deformation (both of which can modulate how a cell interacts with the ECM or an APC) and the spatial organization of TCR microclusters and associated signaling complexes. T-cell spreading following TCR engagement has been show to exhibit a pronounced dependence on substrate stiffness. A. Wahl et. al. (2018) observed a biphasic relationship between substrate stiffness and TCR-mediated spreading, with maximal spreading at intermediate stiffness and reduced spreading on both very soft and extremely rigid substrates. The area increases as the stiffness is increased up to 5 kPa, reaching a maximum of about 300 µm<sup>2</sup>. Thereafter, the cell spread area decreases with increasing stiffness, falling back to roughly 200 µm<sup>2 </sup>for 2 MPa and to less than 150 µm<sup>2</sup> at 7 MPa. The response was dependent on actin polymerization, as inhibition of actin polymerization or Arp2/3-mediated branching prevented spreading, whereas myosin II inhibition did not substantially alter the stiffness dependence. These findings indicate that substrate mechanics can regulate early TCR-associated morphological responses through actin-dependent processes. This biphasic relationship between substrate stiffness and cell spreading was also observed by D. J. Yuan et.al. (2018). In their reports, cells showed the highest spreading area on the 25 kPa gels after 30 mins of seeding. However, this response subsequently shifts, with cell spreading was highest on the 110 kPa substrates at 1 and 4 h time points. 

Mechanical regulation of membrane deformation and architecture may additionally influence the organization and accessibility of TCR signaling molecules along with modulating a T-cells ability to scan its environment and more importantly, APC surface (which is known to be covered by a glycocalyx that has to be breached to get to the pMHC). Discussing the link between substrate stiffness, membrane deformation and TCR-ligand interaction, Hivroz and Saitakis mentioned that " “stiff substrates would allow more deformation of the T lymphocyte membrane and better molecular segregation at the T cell protrusions compared to soft substrates and this, in turn, would result in an increased number of successful interactions between TCR and ligands (anti-CD3 antibodies or pMHC complexes)". Amongst the most studied structures produced through membrane deformation are microvilli, that provide a confined spatial environment that facilitates the organization of signaling molecules. X. Wu & Z. Ye (2025) have written about the effects of microvilli formation and signaling molecule organization, which has been observed in the form of recruitment of TCR, CD4, LFA1, Zap-70, CD2, while excluding CD45 before antigen recognition. The actin cytoskeletal components, especially the ERM (ezrin, radixin and moesin) proteins, play a central role in this preassembly of the TCR complex and its proximal signaling components within microvilli, improving initial antigen recognition efficiency. Aramesh et. al. (2021) demonstrated that nanoscale confinement within approximately 200 and 400 nm pores promoted the formation and stabilization of T-cell microvilli, with pores of 200 nm leading to more pronounced T-cell signaling activation. T cells on these porous surfaces displayed sustained pY and ERK signaling and increased IL-2 secretion and proliferation following TCR stimulation. The porosity of microenvironment seems to have an important difference upon T-cell mechanotransduction than the microenvironment's stiffness. A few other studies (like Hickey et.al., Hui et. al. and Husson et. al.) have put forward the observation that TCR is the main mechanosensing apparatus of a T-cell, with other adhesion receptors playing a supporting by acting as tethering points and applying specific forces that aid in TCR activation. Regarding porosity, however, porous (200 nm), non-activating substrates showed similar pERK levels as non-porous, activating substrates. Substrates with pores of other sizes (20, 100 nm and 1 µm) were not able to produce microvilli with sufficient stability comparable to 200 and 400 nm sized pores. These observations were explained through the idea of "confined geometry" which is at the heart of the kinetic segregation model and is associated with spatial exclusion of large phosphatases like CD45 (∼40 nm is much larger compared to ∼15 nm TCR) from the pores. This idea is supported by imaging experiments which show that CD45 with smaller ectodomain (~7 nm) is able to enter the 200 nm pores, however whether this lead to abrogation of T-cell signaling activation was not reported. 

This idea of "confined geometry" has also been discussed in context of regulation of TCR organization by actin meshworks by Beemiller and Krummel in two of their publications. In their 2010 review, their talk about the role of actin cytoskeletal scaffold in confining extinguished TCRs in zones that does not support signaling, thus establishing a ceiling for TCR signal generation. This may prevent run-away signal propagation and the induction of T-cell apoptosis in response to strong pathogens. They also mention another article by Nguyen et. al. (2008) which reported that ligation of the VLA-4 integrin reduced the actin-dependent rate of micro-cluster centralization, which then increased the time that SLP76 remained associated with LAT in active peripheral microclusters. These findings demonstrate that mechanical and geometrical constraints can potentially enhance TCR signaling by modifying the microscale organization of receptors and phosphatases rather than by directly changing receptor affinity.

##### 4.4.3 SMPs → T-cell motility and arrest following antigen encounter
SMPs may also influence TCR signaling indirectly by modifying T-cell motility, arrest and the dynamics of antigen-presenting-cell encounters. Beemiller & Krummel (2010) mentioned that the actino-myosin cytoskeleton reacts to local integrin levels to allow T cells to adopt one of two crawling modes. M. Saitakis et. al. (2017) observed stiffness-dependent changes in T-cell migration, with higher migration velocities and greater distances travelled on stiffer substrates. They proposed that this response could involve the actin–talin–integrin–ligand mechanical clutch, whereby sufficient mechanical resistance permits talin-dependent reinforcement of integrin adhesions. This observation of higher migration speeds in stiffer substrates was also reported by F. S. Majedi et. al. (2020), however the numbers differed between the two reports. The former found a mean instantaneous velocity of migrating T cells of 12.4 ± 0.3 µm/min (for 0.5 kPa gel), 13.1 ± 0.3 µm/min (for the 6.4 kPa gel) and 15.5 ± 0.4 µm/min (for 100 kPa gels), showing light but significantly increasing speed in higher stiffness. In the study by Majedi et.al., the average speed of T cells crawling within 40 kPa scaffolds (4.7 ± 0.3 μm/min), was higher than within 4 kPa scaffolds (4.0 ± 0.2 μm/min). This difference can be due to multiple factors with the two most prominent ones being a difference in cell types (Saitakis et. al. used CD4+ cells from human PBMC samples while Majedi et.al. used CD4+ cells from OT-II TCR transgenic mice) and microenvironment (2D substrate for Saitakis et. al. and 3D scaffold for Majedi et. al.) Additionally, Saitakis et.al., who showed that the migrational distance also scaled up with increasing stiffness. This pattern was also present in the study of Trenzinger et.al., (who used spatial confinement to apply mechanical forces on T-cells) where cells in non-activating conditions showed increased motility upon confinement, an observation they mentioned was also present in Park et.al. (not included in the final set for data extraction). An intriguing aspect of the effect of confinement and motility was that although resting cells became more motile upon confinement, cells in activating conditions showed a decrease in motility upon application of mechanical forces through confinement. This observation of TCR engagement inducing a stop signal, converting a motile T cell into an arrested cell during antigen recognition, has also been reported by Beemiller et. al. (2012, not part of the final included studies) and Saitakis et.al and indicate towards a mechanism that allows T-cells to better interact with APCs. In the reports of Saitakis et. al., the percentage of arrested T cells in activating conditions was higher on the 100 kPa gels than on the softer ones. Yet, percentage of arrested cells never reached the level obtained on glass coated with aCD3+aCD28+ICAM-1. Moreover, aCD3 coupled to 0.5 and 6.4 kPa did not induce any significant deceleration of lymphoblasts, while it did induce deceleration of non-arrested lymphoblasts when coupled to 100 kPa gels. These studies demonstrate a coordination between TCR activation and migration, and provides an intriguing avenue for SMPs to indirectly influence antigen recognition by altering  the frequency, duration and geometry of encounters with APCs. 

##### 4.4.4 Application of mechanical upon TCR by the cytoskeleton
As discussed in section 1.2, TCR acts as a mechanosensor with mechanical forces altering the behavior of TCR-pMHC interaction. The magnitude of force experienced by the TCR is not simply a consequence of ligand binding but also is actively regulated by the cytoskeletal architecture of the T cell. Early mechanobiological studies have estimated forces of approximately 10–20 pN at individual TCR–pMHC complexes, with changes in force affecting TCR sensitivity to antigen. TCRs are mechanically coupled to the actin cytoskeleton through the CD3 complex, allowing actin flows and cytoskeletal remodeling to generate directional forces across the receptor. Work by Beemiller & Krummel (2010) and X. Wu & Z. Ye (2025) emphasizes that the orientation of these forces is important: shear forces associated with cellular movement and microvillus protrusion can promote TCR–pMHC engagement, whereas opposing actin flow can promote bond dissociation. Thus, mechanical loading may contribute both to antigen recognition and to the kinetic proofreading of receptor–ligand interactions. This mechanical loading also seems to be central to a T-cells ability to interpret its mechanical environment (as discussed in section 4.4.1) as reported by Hui et.al. According to them, T-cells have the ability to sense the substrate stiffness and modulate the internally generated cytoskeletal forces as a function of substrate stiffness and these observed forces are largely a direct consequence of TCR–ligand binding

### Section 5: Discussion and future direction
This scoping review was written to map the current available evidence linking the mechanical properties of the thymic stroma/substrate with the thymic selection and other associated developmental processes. The information synthesized from the obtained literature demonstrates that mechanical forces are relevant to several crucial important aspects of thymic selection and development, most prominently, TCR signaling. However, the evidence is highly heterogeneous with respect to cell type, developmental stage, mechanical stimulus and experimental system. Most importantly, there remains a substantial distinction between evidence demonstrating that T cells can sense and respond to mechanical cues and evidence demonstrating that the mechanical properties of the thymic stroma regulate thymocyte selection in vivo.

The study by Jeffreys et. al. (2025) provides the clearest evidence within the included literature that the mechanical properties of a thymus-mimetic extracellular environment can influence T-lineage differentiation. The evidence concerning mechanical regulation of thymocyte selection (Hong et. al. 2018 being the primary source) is particularly interesting because it demonstrates that selection itself is mechanically regulated, but does not yet establish the thymic stroma as the source of the relevant mechanical cue. Substrate stiffness, viscoelasticity, confinement and substrate porosity has been shown to influence TCR-associated phosphorylation, calcium signaling, spreading and activation in mature T-cells. These effects frequently depend on actomyosin contractility, demonstrating that the cytoskeleton can act as an intermediary between extracellular mechanical conditions and receptor signaling. These parameters may influence cell spreading, migration, cytoskeletal tension, receptor organization and force transmission in different ways. Consequently, describing the thymic mechanical environment using a single stiffness value may be insufficient to capture the relevant biological information. Similar to the multi-factorial nature of SMPs, there are many routes by which these properties can influence intracellular signaling of thymocytes/T-cells. Mechanosensitive ion channels (such as Piezo1), YAP-dependent transcription, Notch signaling and mechanical transmission through the LINC complex and nuclear lamina provide potential mechanisms by which extracellular mechanical conditions could influence intracellular functions and behaviors such as gene expression and lineage decision-making.

The primary conclusion that emerges from this endeavor is therefore not that the thymic SMPs have already been established as modulators of thymic selection but that several critical mechanistic processes and relevant components in this relationship are supported by the literature. These include mechanical regulation of T-cell lineage differentiation, role of mechanical forces in influencing TCR-pMHC bond behavior, cytoskeletal control of important surrounding cellular functions such as migrational velocity and cell spreading, mechanotransduction through integrins and Piezo1. The potential convergence of these mechanisms provides a plausible basis for SMPs to influence thymic selection, but as it is evident from the final synthesis in each of the EMs, there is a significant absence of direct experimental evidence related to many central questions. This highlights the novel nature of this field and acts as a rich source of experiments that can be undertaken to address the vast gaps in knowledge in this field. 

#### 5.1 Two most prominent knowledge gaps
Amongst the many knowledge gaps that were left following the final synthesis, two stood out considerably. First is the dearth of experiments that used thymocytes as cell models, with most of them using mature T-cells instead. This is crucial to discuss in depth because a shallow picture of the future works can be constructed around the idea of "just use thymocytes instead of mature T-cells and repeat the relevant mechanobiological experiments", which can be misleading without pondering about the reason of the absence of thymocytes from these studies. The second prominent gap is the lack of any relevant data in studies which made it to the final stages of this review and connect changes in thymic SMPs due to aging and disease with thymic selection and developmental changes.

##### 5.1.1 Thymocyte models being almost absence from the studies
Being part of the same lineage, thymocytes do share many important features relevant to our discussion here such as the core TCR signaling scaffold (ZAP-70, LAT, SLP-76, PLC-γ1 are expressed in both and their basic wiring is quite similar), LFA-1/ICAM-1 adhesion and integrin outside-in signaling operate in both populations, Calcium-NFAT axis is shared and cytoskeletal reorganization upon TCR engagement follows similar logic in both contexts.
Although thymocytes have been used for experiments since the 1960s, mature T-cells (either primary or secondary) have been much extensively studied and used for such experiments. The possible explanations regarding the scarce use of thymocytes include:
- **Having low accessibility**: thymocytes require animal sacrifice and thymus dissection. Additionally, the human thymus involutes substantially by early adulthood, making primary human thymocyte studies practically dependent on pediatric surgical samples that is a rare and ethically constrained resource. On the other hand, mature primary T-cells come from blood draws and secondary cell lines like Jurkat grow indefinitely;
- **Having low ex vivo survival**: DP thymocytes undergo rapid spontaneous apoptosis when removed from the thymic microenvironment, often within hours. Thus, long mechanobiological assays (such as overnight stiffness priming and extended TFM measurements) are simply not compatible with this cell type in standard culture conditions;
- **No intact DP cell lines have been developed**: there are no widely used immortalized cell lines that faithfully recapitulate DP thymocyte signaling with intact selection machinery; 
- **Apparent historical momentum**: T-cell mechanobiology seems to have grown out of the adaptive immunity activation literature, where the question has been mainly about mature effector T-cells and their interaction with APCs at peripheral sites, making the cells model which represent them more common than thymocytes. 
These reasons provide some idea of why thymocytes have been understudied and under-utilized and why mature T-cell models dominate the experimental realm. It is also important to keep the following points in mind regarding the difference between mature T-cells (and secondary cells lines) and thymocytes for sake of caution in interpreting the results of the studies in the final synthesis: 
- **Signaling threshold architecture**: Thymocytes are undergoing analog signal integration for the purpose of fate decisions with dramatic consequences (death by neglect, apoptosis from negative selection, Treg commitment). Their TCR signaling has been observed to operate at much lower activation threshold than mature T-cells, which have already been calibrated through selection. A substrate stiffness effect that is sub-threshold for mature T-cell activation may be entirely sufficient to shift thymocyte fate. Quantitative extrapolation is therefore unreliable even when qualitative pathway logic is shared.
-  **SHP-1 expression levels**:  Thymocytes show substantially higher levels of SHP-1 (a phosphatase that dephosphorylates targets of kinases like TCR-ζ chain, Lck, ZAP-70 and LAT) expression than mature peripheral T-cells. In the well-established kinetic proofreading model, it's what makes the serial triggering model work at low-affinity ligands. This means the mechanobiological modulation of TCR dwell time (which is exactly what catch-bond mechanics affects) plays out on a different phosphatase backdrop in thymocytes.
- **Dual CD4/CD8 co-receptor expression on DP thymocytes**: DP thymocytes co-express both co-receptors simultaneously, which is not recapitulated by any mature T-cell or secondary cell lines. CD4 and CD8 each recruit Lck, and their simultaneous presence changes the geometry of early signaling complex formation at the membrane. This is mechanobiologically non-trivial because the spatial arrangement of signaling molecules at the contact interface differs fundamentally.
- **Cell size, cortical stiffness, and spreading capacity**: DP thymocytes are small, round cells that do not spread extensively on substrates. Most mechanobiological assays (traction force microscopy, pillar deflection, IS area measurement) are calibrated around cells that spread substantially. So, the mechanical read-out geometry that might work for a Jurkat cell forming a large IS will most likely not translate cleanly to a DP thymocyte.
- **Cell model-specific confounds**: Secondary cell lines are commonly used in experiments because of ease in culturing and propagation. However, the same specific alterations in their machinery which allow these positive points also can cause different behaviors in experiments which have to be kept in mind while extrapolating the data. Jurkat cells lack PTEN, making PI3K constitutively active, which is deeply entangled with mechanosensing as it feeds into integrin activation, actin dynamics, and AKT-mediated cytoskeletal regulation. Any Jurkat-based mechanobiology result involving substrate rigidity sensing or IS formation must be treated with specific caution because the PI3K axis that normally gates these responses is constitutively derepressed.

##### 5.1.2 Lack of relevant data in the EM-4
Amongst the main findings of this scoping review is that no studies meeting the decided inclusion criteria addressed the question of how thymic selection and associated developmental processes are influenced during aging and disease through alterations in SMPs. Two possible explanations can be given for this finding:
- The question has not been asked, i.e. aging/diseased thymic mechanobiology simply hasn't been studied yet, reflecting a major gap in research efforts;
- The question has been asked but not in mechanobiological terms. This points towards the presence of a significant thymic involution literature (adipose replacement, TEC loss, ECM changes with age) that didn't surface in the review because the studies did not use similar mechanobiological vocabulary or outcome measures.
Both of these have some backing in the literature. Some studies have explored mechanical properties of thymic tissue (some are mentioned in section 1) but they are still few in number (especially compared to other tissues like liver, bone, etc.) and do not focus on processes associated to thymocyte development. On the other hand, there are considerable number of studies focused on the consequences of aging and disease (primarily due to the clinical significance of thymic involution and novel T-cell therapies) but they do put emphasis on SMPs and hence, do not contain the terms that are searched for during the screening process. Based on these point, it is evident that considerable is possible for linking these two research niches and this effort likely holds great potential due to the benefits of understanding and leveraging T-cell development in clinical settings.

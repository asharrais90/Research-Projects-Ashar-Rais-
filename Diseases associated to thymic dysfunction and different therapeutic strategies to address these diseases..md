##### Thymus May Be Critical for Longevity and Cancer Immunotherapy Response


The relationship between thymic dysfunction and disease is particularly clear in several monogenic disorders. Mutations affecting genes required for thymic development can cause profound defects in T‑cell production. A prominent example is 22q11.2 deletion/DiGeorge syndrome, in which thymic hypoplasia or complete athymia can produce severe T‑cell immunodeficiency. The phenotype is variable, but complete athymia can result in profound failure of T‑cell reconstitution [PMC].

Other genetic abnormalities affect thymic selection rather than simply thymic size. AIRE is particularly important because it promotes the expression of peripheral tissue‑restricted antigens by medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs), enabling developing thymocytes with potentially dangerous self‑reactivity to encounter those antigens during negative selection. Defective AIRE function, as occurs in autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 (APS‑1/APECED), therefore compromises central tolerance and produces multi‑organ autoimmunity. More generally, genetic perturbations affecting thymic antigen presentation and selection can alter the balance between deletion of autoreactive T cells and preservation of useful TCR specificities [PubMed].

TCR signaling itself provides another important connection between thymic selection and disease. TCR signal strength is a major determinant of positive and negative selection as well as Treg development. Recent synthesis of this literature indicates that partial attenuation of proximal TCR signaling can produce an altered TCR repertoire enriched for autoreactivity while simultaneously impairing Treg generation, creating a phenotype that can progress from immune deficiency to latent or overt autoimmunity. ZAP‑70 provides a particularly well‑characterized example: severe loss of ZAP‑70 function causes immunodeficiency, whereas hypomorphic alterations can disturb thymic selection and predispose to autoimmune and inflammatory disease [Annual Reviews].

The connection becomes more complex in polygenic autoimmune diseases. Type 1 diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis and myasthenia gravis have all been associated with abnormalities in thymic tolerance or Treg biology, but thymic dysfunction generally represents one component of a larger network involving genetic susceptibility, peripheral tolerance, environmental triggers and immune regulation. Thus, it is usually inappropriate to describe these diseases simply as consequences of failed negative selection [Frontiers].

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is particularly interesting because pathological changes in the thymus are common in subsets of patients. Thymic hyperplasia and thymoma are associated particularly with acetylcholine‑receptor‑antibody‑positive disease, whereas thymic atrophy is more characteristic of some late‑onset cases. Thymic epithelial abnormalities and altered antigen presentation have therefore been investigated as potential contributors to defective tolerance [PMC]. Thymic epithelial tumors are also associated with a broad spectrum of autoimmune manifestations, including MG, pure red‑cell aplasia and Good syndrome, illustrating how disruption of thymic architecture can simultaneously affect tolerance and immune competence [PubMed].

Thymic dysfunction can therefore produce two apparently opposite clinical outcomes. Insufficient thymic function can lead to inadequate generation of naïve T cells and immunodeficiency, whereas abnormal thymic selection can allow autoreactive T cells to escape or impair the generation of appropriate Tregs, contributing to autoimmunity. In practice, the two phenomena can coexist because a damaged thymus may simultaneously have reduced thymopoiesis and defective selection.

## Therapeutic strategies

Therapeutic approaches can broadly be divided into replacement/restoration of thymic function, correction of the underlying thymic defect, and suppression or reprogramming of the resulting autoimmune response.

For congenital athymia, the most direct strategy is thymus transplantation. In complete DiGeorge syndrome, cultured postnatal thymic tissue can be transplanted, commonly into the quadriceps, where recipient progenitors migrate into the graft and undergo thymopoiesis. A 2025 systematic review identified thymopoiesis and development of naïve T cells in most reported recipients, with relatively infrequent graft failure or rejection [PubMed]. This is particularly important conceptually for thymic biology because transplantation demonstrates that restoration of an appropriate stromal environment can restore T‑cell development even when the recipient cannot provide a functional thymus.

For autoimmune diseases, treatment is currently much less directly targeted at thymic selection. Conventional therapy generally acts downstream by suppressing pathological immune responses. Approaches include corticosteroids, conventional immunosuppressants, biologics targeting inflammatory cytokines or lymphocyte populations, and increasingly therapies designed to produce more selective immune tolerance. The limitation is that these treatments generally do not repair the original defect in central tolerance, and broad immunosuppression can increase susceptibility to infection [Nature].

Thymectomy provides an unusual example in which modifying the thymus itself can be therapeutic. It is an established component of management for selected patients with MG, particularly thymoma‑associated disease and appropriate non‑thymomatous AChR‑antibody‑positive MG. However, thymectomy should not be interpreted simply as “removing a defective thymus”; its effects are complex, and autoimmune manifestations can persist or even arise following surgery [PubMed].

More ambitious approaches aim to restore or reconstruct central tolerance. These include manipulation of thymic epithelial cells, restoration of AIRE‑dependent antigen presentation, Treg‑based therapies, antigen‑specific tolerance, thymic regeneration and tissue‑engineered/artificial thymic niches. These approaches remain substantially more experimental than conventional immunosuppression. The underlying rationale is attractive: rather than continually suppressing autoreactive lymphocytes after they have escaped into the periphery, one could potentially restore the developmental environment that prevents those cells from emerging in the first place. Current reviews identify thymic regeneration and thymus‑based therapies as important areas for future development [Wiley].

This distinction is particularly relevant to mechanobiology. Most existing therapeutic approaches manipulate cellular, molecular or immunological components of thymic function, rather than the physical properties of the thymic microenvironment. If stromal stiffness, ECM organization, mechanical confinement or cytoskeletal mechanotransduction influence thymocyte selection, then abnormal stromal mechanics could represent an additional, largely unexplored layer of thymic dysfunction. Conversely, restoring an appropriate mechanical microenvironment could eventually become part of a strategy for reconstructing thymic function.

Thus, the therapeutic literature currently provides a useful conceptual hierarchy: established treatments can replace absent thymic function or suppress downstream autoimmunity, while emerging approaches seek to regenerate the thymus or restore tolerance. A major unresolved question is whether the physical properties of the thymic microenvironment themselves contribute to disease‑associated defects in selection—and whether those properties could eventually be manipulated therapeutically. This is precisely where a mechanobiological understanding of thymic selection could complement the predominantly molecular and cellular models of thymic dysfunction.

The immune system is one of the most important parts of our physiological functioning as it plays the crucial of defending other systems from things that might damage the healthy functioning of those systems. T-cells are important players of the immune system and their role mainly revolves around building defenses against cellular threats such as cancerous and virus-infected cells. Being part of the adaptive branch of the immune system, T-cells require interaction with a specific pathogen/antigen to become activated and get into the defensive action. This immunofunctioning of T-cells depends mainly upon the membrane bound T-cell receptors (TCR), which bind processed antigens (derived from both, self and foreign proteins) displayed by the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC). Additional surface proteins such as CD4, CD8, PD-1, B7, etc. also play important roles in functioning and identity determination of T-cells. 

The production of T-cells occurs in regions where hematopoietic stem cells reside (fetal liver and bone marrow in adults) and from these regions. In humans, early thymus-seeding populations are commonly characterised by expression of **CD34**, with populations such as CD34^hi CD45⁺ CD7⁺ progenitors showing T-, NK- and B-cell potential. CD34⁺CD7⁻ progenitors with broader lymphoid, myeloid and erythroid potential have also been detected in human thymus, illustrating the heterogeneity of the cells capable of contributing to thymopoiesis. the T-cell precursors (TCPs) travel to a primary lymphoid organ called the thymus for further maturation (which is the production of specific subsets of T-cells like CD4+ T-helpers, CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells, Treg cells, etc.) and training. The Arrival (Pro-T1): The moment they enter the thymus, they rapidly turn on CD7 but lack CD5, forming the CD34+ CD7+ CD5− (proT1) stage. The Transition (ProT2): Once inside the thymic environment, Notch signaling triggers them to turn on CD5, advancing them to the CD34+ CD7+ CD5+ (proT2) stage. 

 Anatomically, this organ is composed of multiple regions--capsule (outermost), sub-capsular region, cortex, cortico-medullary junction (CMJ) and medulla (innermost)--with each of these regions having specific populations of cells (mainly thymic epithelial cells/TECs, fibroblasts and antigen presenting cells/APCs) and mechanical properties (with the extracellular matrix/ECM composed mainly of collagen I/III/IV, fibronectin). Importantly, the thymus undergoes a peculiar form of development called as thymic involution that starts around puberty and results in the thymic stroma becoming dominated by fatty tissue instead of functional components mentioned above. The TCPs migrate to the thymus through the influence of specific chemokines (following which they are referred to as thymocytes) and undergo training, which is the process of allowing only immunocompetent (can recognize MHC complexes) and self-tolerant (do not reactive against self-antigens) T-cells to exit the thymus and travel though the body. 
 
 Problems in the training process often lead to auto-immune disorders and immunodeficiencies, pointing towards it importance. T-cell training begins in the cortex as the thymocytes migrate towards the medulla and this composed of two distinct parts- positive selection (in which thymocytes that are unable to recognize/bind to MHC complexes die through apoptosis to ensure immunocompetence) and negative selection (in which thymocytes that bind self peptides-MHC/self-pMHC with too high affinity undergo apoptosis to ensure self-tolerance). This review is focused more on studies on negative selection as its central process has a greater contribution of mechanical forces and signals compared to positive selection (where the binding or non-binding to MHC is biochemical in nature). Till date most studies have focused on the cellular and biochemical properties of the thymus and their roles in negative selection, however with the recently expanding focus on the integration of mechanobiology with immunology, many groups have now started to explore the role of mechanical signals in immunological processes. T-cells have especially received a lot of attention for these mechanoimmunological investigations as the TCR seems to act as a mechanosensor when probing its environment, with seminal works such as Kim *et.al.* (2012) proposing a "catch-bond" mechanism (a bond which strengthens as more force is applied to it, upto a specific degree) to explain TCR dynamics. Studying these concepts is crucial for understanding how binding affinity of TCR to self-pMHC is converted to information regarding the thymocytes' survival and work regarding this has shown that mechanotransductional pathways are activated downstream of TCR activation. For this selection process, both the cellular and non-cellular components of the thymic stroma are critical as the former (mainly, medullary TECs/mTECs and APCs) contain the self-pMHC and the latter form the physical scaffold which not only influence cellular density (which then influences the chance of thymocytes interacting with stromal cells) but also can affect a thymocytes signaling networks and cytoskeletal systems that alter the mechanotransductional signal being produced by the TCR.


### Abstract
The research and applications of regenerative therapies is amongst the fastest developing areas in biology in this century, with mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) based therapies representing a significant portion of this field. MSCs, through their healing promoting capabilities, are being utilised in treatment of various ailments, primarily through injecting these cells into a person’s body following which they travel to targeted tissue through a specific process called stem cell homing. Unfortunately, this process suffers from a heavy drawback of low efficiency, i.e., only a small percentage of injected cells reaching the intended target. Many approaches have been explored to deal with this drawback, including targeted delivery. However, there is still a lot of discover about the homing paradigm and how we can increase its efficiency, and one of the main unexplored aspects of this process is the role of mechanotransductional pathways in MSCs. The LINC (Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton) complex, present in the nuclear membrane, is an important component of these pathways and it functions as a direct physical connection between nuclear components and cytoskeletal elements, which then connect to extracellular matrix (ECM) through plasma membrane complexes. While some studies have investigated LINC’s role in MSC migration and lineage commitments, no study till date has been published regarding its role in stem cell homing paradigm, and none has used these novel technologies of organ-on-chip (which combine microfluidics and tissue engineering to address specific drawbacks of traditional models). The project proposed below, planned for a four-year graduate program, involves the creation of bone marrow-&-liver-on chip model to study LINC’s role in MSC migration to an injured region under the influence of chemokines and in chromatin-associated processes that influence MSC’s cell fate decisions after reaching the injured region. Expected outcomes of this project include a better understanding in how LINC transmits forces to nuclear structures in specific steps of the homing paradigm and the effects of these forces upon chromatin dynamics and gene expression changes, which might result in practical steps to increase MSC homing efficiency (in combination with other methods mentioned before) and produce real-world benefits for patients worldwide.

### Introduction
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are defined by the following criteria**1**—having the potential to differentiate into adipogenic, should be adherent to plastic under normal culture conditions, chondrogenic and osteogenic lineages, presence of CD70, CD93 and CD105 markers, and absence of markers for other cell types (like hematopoietic stem cell marker CD34 and pan-leukocyte marker CD45). It has been observed that following certain types of injuries in different tissues (liver, for eg.), bone marrow MSCs exit their residence and migrate to the injured region under the influence of specific chemokines and promote healing of the damage tissue through secretion of cytokines and production of deficient cell types**2,3**. This process through which these cells that are circulating in the blood stream travel to different tissues and enter into specific niches is commonly referred to as stem cell homing, and can be divided into five steps—1. Rolling, 2. Activation, 3. Arrest, 4. Transmigration, and 5. Migration**4**. In addition to the natural physiological response to tissue injury, this homing paradigm is utilised following in regenerative medicine-related procedures to augment our body’s healing capabilities**5**. However, when MSCs are introduced into a person’s systematic circulatory system, only a small fraction of the cell population is observed to home to the region of interest, with a significant fraction of the injected population getting trapped in other tissues (mainly, lung and liver). This represents an addressable obstacle to improvement of MSC-based regenerative therapies and multiple methods (such as direct administration, drug pretreatment, etc.) are being explored by different groups to try and increase the homing efficiency**2,4**. As these MSC-based therapies hold great potential in treatment of numerous ailments (such as orthopaedic disorders**6**, cardiac injuries**7**, auto-immune disorders**8**, etc.), there are significant real-world benefits of exploring MSC homing (with the MSC market size is estimated at USD 4.59 billion in 2025, and is expected to reach USD 8.19 billion by 2030**9**). As such, different underexplored aspects of the MSC homing paradigm become attractive sources of research endeavours, and this proposal, focussed on specific mechanobiological processes of MSC homing, has been developed through this thought process.

A protein complex found in the nuclear membrane named as LINC (linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton) serves as a direct connection from nuclear components (specifically, lamina and chromatin) to cytoskeletal components, and through these, even to the ECM**10,11**. This complex allows the transmission of physical signals to the nucleus in much shorter timeframes (in milliseconds) when compared to the pathways with soluble signalling proteins (in seconds)**12**. Its main components are two types of proteins**13**—KASH domain-containing proteins (represented by Nesprins) and SUN domain containing proteins (represented by SUN1/2)—in addition to other LINC-associated proteins like emerin14 (that allows the complex to be associated with the lamina). Cellular functions of LINC complex include maintaining proper nuclear orientation and structure during cell migration**15,16**, being involved in DNA damage repair pathways**17** and epigenetic processes**18**, and influencing cell fate decisions (especially in MSCs, adipogenic lineage differentiation was found to be linked to LINC functioning)**19**. All these findings point towards the importance of LINC’s role in cellular processes under different contexts, however no group till date has studied its role in stem cell homing, and investigating it might be of significant benefit in MSC homing. One method of studying this topic is through the use of organ-on-chip (OoC) technology, which combines microfluidics and tissue engineering to develop recapitulations of specific tissues on polymer-based chip platforms**20**. These new kinds of models offer the benefit of mimicking the physiological systems much more faithfully than 2-D plates/flasks and having much less ethical issues compared to live animal models**21**. Consequently, the use of this technology has increased a lot in the past two decades and it has been used to study topics such as cancer metastasis**22**, drug screening**23** and recapitulation of complex tissue niches like those found in the bone marrow**22,24**, making it an appropriate choice to use in the exploration of LINC’s role in the MSC homing paradigm, something which to my knowledge has not been done till date.

### Research Question

**Does affecting LINC functioning alter MSC migration capabilities and lineage commitments during the MSC homing paradigm?**

### Research Methodology

The entire research work for this project can be divided into two main objectives: (I) Making a Bone Marrow-Liver OoC (BLOC); (II) using this model to probe LINC functioning in MSC homing.

#### (Objective-I) Designing the BLOC:

·        The material for chips will be PDMS as it is the standard for creation of prototype chips, and these will be designed using SU-8 wafers through the soft photolithography method as mentioned in**25**. The regions surrounding the tissue compartments will have pores of 8-10 μm to allow transmigration of cells**26,27**;

·        BM compartment: Cell types—MSCs (Y201 secondary cell line, established for use in LINC experiments for its ease of genetic engineering**28**) and fibroblasts (HS-27a cell line**29**, for support of MSCs). Matrix—Collagen-I coated polyacrylamide gel with stiffness of ~5 kPa**30**. This will be the initial residence for the MSCs from where they will migrate to the liver compartment through chemotactic migration;

·        Liver compartment and injury simulation: Liver was chosen as the recipient compartment for multiple reasons such as the physiological relevance of MSC homing to liver**31**, presence of well-established studies about creation of liver niches on OOC**32** and lack of requirement for external mechanical forces (like those needed to mimic breathing and heartbeats). Cell types—Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (TRP-3 cell line, for providing mechanical cues for transmigrated MSCs) and hepatocytes (HepaRG cell line). Matrix—Gel-PEG and with the stiffness ~1.3 kPa**33**. CCl4 (a potent hepatotoxin which increases cellular levels of oxidative radicals, particularly in hepatocytes) will be used to simulate acute injury**34** as done in these studies and will be injected into the compartment after both—BM and liver tissues—have matured to a certain extent. The expression of chemokines such as SDF-1α/CXCL-12 is expected to increase due to CCl4**35**, and these will induce chemotaxis in MSCs**31**. Levels of the chemokine gradient can be analysed by taking media samples from different compartments and using ELISA to analyse quantities of the chemokine of interest;

#### (Objective-II) Probing LINC functioning:

Two main aspects of LINC functioning that will be focussed on for this project are—force transmission during MSC movement (especially during transmigration) and its effects upon chromatin dynamics and how it influences MSC fate decision. Perturbation experiments (such as the use of siRNAs**38**, overexpression of dominant negative constructs of LINC components**13**, and CRISPR-based disruptions of LINC components’ genes**39**) will be performed to investigate how disruption of LINC’s form and function might affect MSC homing paradigm.

·        Tools to study force transmission: Molecular force sensors such as Nesprin2G-FRET**40** and NuTS1/2**41** sensors which will give us a profile of forces acting upon the complex during cell migration. Cells will also be imaged during transmigration using fluorescence microscopy to study the link between LINC and nuclear deformation in this process. For imaging, Lamins antibodies attached with fluorescent proteins**42** will be used to visualise nuclear shape.

·        Tools to study chromatin dynamics and gene expression: Techniques such as ATAC-seq (for genome-wide chromatin accessibility) and ChIP-seq (for specific epigenetic marks like H3K9me3 at loci associated to MSC fate decisions**19**) will be performed. The MSCs that have migrated to the liver compartment can be isolated using flow cytometry-based sorting (using MSC markers like CD-70**4**). Additionally, RNA-seq will be performed to analyse the expression levels of mainly genes associated with MSC fate decisions. At specific time intervals, media from the liver compartment will be analysed to study the concentration of MSC-secreted signalling compounds involved in tissue healing through the use of mass spectrometry/ELISA/western blotting. Finally, after a set number of days, cells will be taken from the liver compartment to analyse MSC differentiation into different lineages.

#### Planned timeline for a four-year graduate project at MBI:

·        Year-1: Attendance of relevant coursework and seminars, focus on literature search, simulations (using CAD, for eg.) and discussions to finalise the chip design, and procurement of necessary materials for the initial steps such as chip production;

·        Year-2: Training for relevant sophisticated techniques (fluorescent microscopy, ATAC-seq, etc.), synthesis of the chips, optimisation of each of the compartments and their combination to develop the BM-liver chip (and for liver, optimisation of the CCl4-based injury model), and optimisation of genetically modified MSC-model cells;

·        Year-3: Studies focussed on probing LINC complex’s role in MSC migration (using FRET-based force sensors and imaging of nuclear deformation) and commitment to different lineages (chromatin dynamics and gene expression-based studies);

·        Year-4: Addressing of any experimental gaps, participation in conferences for project results communication and thesis completion.   

#### Expected Outcomes, Limitations and Future Developments

This proposal outlines a project which will be first of its kind as to the best of my knowledge. While, both stem cell homing and LINC functioning have been investigated before, this was done either using 2-D cell cultures or animal models**27,40**, no other group before has either studied LINC’s role in MSC homing or used the OOC models for stem cell homing or LINC studies. This represents the considerable strength of this project as it adds a novel dimension to topics that have been studied that although have been extensively, still hold treasures of important knowledge43. The expected outcomes through this project can be categorised into two domains. The first domain consists of results obtained from wild-type/control form of LINC, and this will include results regarding the force transmissive capabilities (in the form of FRET-based force profiles, fluorescence-based images of nuclear shape) of this complex. The second domain consists results obtained from the LINC perturbation experiments that will shed light on the dependence of MSC upon this complex during the homing process and also the chromatin dynamics influencing capabilities of this complex (in the form of data from ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, expression of healing promoting signalling molecules).

The results from this project depend heavily on how much the BLOC model faithfully replicates the physiological conditions of our body, and consequently making this aspect, the source of major limitations of this project. Through the use of secondary cell lines (for their ease of culturing and genetic modifications), keeping the cell in simple compartments with the bare minimum requirements for recapitulation (for the sake of practical feasibility), the conditions of these compartments deviate away from those found in our organs, adding unknown variables to the results. Additionally, there is the possibility that even revealing LINC complex’s important role in the homing procedure, utilising it for clinical developments would not feasible, decreasing the significance of this proposed study.   

Similar to the limitations, the future developments for this project also revolve around the configuration of the OoC model. This points towards the next possible steps following the successful completion of this project. These steps include making the existing more faithful physiological conditions (mainly by addition of more resident cell types like immune cells and using matrices with controlled variation in properties), adding more compartments (such as lung, gut etc.) and use of primary cells/iPSCs instead of secondary cell lines. Another promising project which can be derived from the one proposed here is to study the role of LINC complex in homing of hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) with OoC. As HSC homing is central to procedure of bone marrow transplantation and also suffers from an efficiency issue like MSC homing, this project will have the same thought process of studying the underexplored aspects (which includes mechanobiological functioning) to try different methods of increasing cell homing efficiency. With the bone marrow transplantation procedure becoming more common in the treatment for blood disorders like leukaemia, this project holds great potential to benefit a considerably large group of patients worldwide, whose survival depends of the success of this procedure.

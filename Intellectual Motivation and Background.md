### Early days:
“The cell is the basic and functional unit of life”. One of the most well-known statements in the scientific realm, which when I first came to learn during middle school, did not make much of a mental dent in comparison to questions of how to win the football game in the evening and what is for dinner after the game. As times progressed, and the inherent responsibilities and pressures of life—including an increased frequency of the questions of “what kind of person do you want be?” and “what do you want to work on as a career?”—started to dim the interest for winning football matches in me, I began formulating my own answers to these practically relevant and deeply philosophical questions. My proper entry into the field of biology was not based purely due to interest in it but also because I had no desire to take mathematics as the major subject, or even have it as a significant part of my studies. Around this time, I came across that famed quote about cells again in lectures about cellular biology, and this time with slightly more mature mind, I seriously started to entertain this question. “If these tiny cells are this important to life, how are they necessary when I am dribbling on a football field?" Through these fascinations, my studies started to be driven by internal curiosity instead of a chase of external metrics like grades. This amplified the quality and quantity of my intellectual adventures and led me to explore a vast range of concepts, from Robert Hooke’s work on visualizing cells for the first time to cutting-edge interdisciplinary fields of nanomedicine and quantum biology. But after studying the subject for a considerable amount of time and consequently developing a sense of intellectual humility, I realized that truly working to understand such a rich and complex field is hopeless without first having a burning desire to do so. Following this internal, I started looking for external guidance, particularly into the works and lives of the great minds of different eras—such as Nikola Tesla, Gregor Mendel, Ramanujan, Alan Turing, Ibn Al-Haytham—who have contributed immensely in shaping their fields to what they are today. In some time, due these deep dives into all the material I could find (and handle) and meticulous contemplation, the answer to “what do you want to work on as a career?” was in front of me, and it was to become a researcher of cellular biology.

### Experiences in BSc course:
The next two phases of my life as a researcher (BSc and MSc courses) were spent refining my understanding and answers to the questions mentioned above, with both of these phases being greatly different to each other. The transition from high-school to college (BSc in biochemistry in the Aligarh Muslim University or AMU) was unexpectedly complicated and was shifted from 2020 to 2021, with the whole world taking a ‘sick leave’ due to something which a lot of people do not even consider to be alive. To be admitted into the esteemed biochemistry department in AMU (whose Faculty of Life Sciences was ranked 'number one Faculty of the Nation' in Times Higher Education-India ranking 2018), a competitive entrance where tens of thousands of students from all over India competed had to be passed and I managed to obtain the 3rd rank in it. This BSc phase, spent mostly during the lockdown, was characterized by intense theoretical explorations of different fields, some remotely connected to cellular biology. Reading about the structural mechanism of rotation of ATPase pump from Lehninger’s Principles of Biochemistry and the philosophy of cellular biology from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, watching documentaries about nanomedicine and biomechatronics, and having delightful discussions with wonderful friends, made this a special time for my intellectual growth and helped me develop a particular interest for interdisciplinary fields. Some practical works generated out of these interests included a review publication on the mechanotransductional connection between extracellular matrix (ECM) and cell cycle and development of ideas (about bio-mimetic artificial matrix and quantum-dots for theranostic purposes) for university-wide competitions. Despite of the growth in theoretical knowledge, the growth in technical skills was lacking, as the online coursework was completely lacking in experimental training except in the final year, when in-person classes were reopened. This insufficiency was fortunately sorted out during my MSc course, for which I was admitted into the prestigious King Abdullah University of Science & Technology (KAUST), considered to be the best home to higher education in the Middle East by Times Higher Education rankings.

### Experiences in MSc course:
From an academic perspective, coming to KAUST was a fantastic experience mainly due to the culture that put emphasis on the quality of learning and research. The course work gave me opportunities to critically analyze research articles and learn about many advanced concepts—such as cytoskeleton’s role in cell migration, cell stemness and epigenetic control of this property and the use of imaging techniques in biological research—from world renowned experts like Prof. Valerio Orlando, Prof. Jasmeen Merzaban, Prof. Satoshi Habuchi and Prof. Christian Jensen. My primary experimental experience was obtained during my MSc thesis, done in the Cell Migration and Signaling lab (PI: Prof. Merzaban). In the initial stages, filled with ambition and wonderful ideas being discussed in the courses, I began a project to study the role of the LINC (linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton) complex in mechanotransductional signaling in the stem cell homing paradigm. The primary question underlying this project was whether the dramatic changes in mechanical forces experienced by a stem cell during homing affect cell survival and lineage formation through the LINC complex functioning. However, before any publishable findings were made, this project halted due to certain unforeseen circumstances. Nevertheless, I learned multiple techniques and critical skills, including designing adaptable experimental frameworks and the resilience needed to complete the experiments despite the obstacles. And just like the well-known quote, _"Failure is simply the opportunity to begin again, this time more intelligently."_, I moved on to a collaborative project with Dr. Naif Almontashiri’s lab at Taibah University that explored how a CD34 mutation alters hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) functioning and consequently influence G6PDD severity. What made this project unique was that, no other team had explored the pathogenicity associated with the CD34 protein. Working under the mentorship of Dr. Asma Al-Amoodi, to characterize the disruption in CD34 and HSC functioning, I:
- Engineered HL60 cells via lentiviral transduction to express different variants of CD34 and G6PD;
- Quantified CD34 expression using RT-qPCR, immunoblotting and flow cytometry;
- Analyzed CD34 glycosylation through stains and enzymes-based assays;
- Studied protein-protein interactions using immunoprecipitation and molecular dynamics simulations;
- Imaged cells using confocal microscopy;
- Mapped cell migration via microfluidics-based cell rolling procedure.

As this work represents only a small portion of a larger clinical case, it did not lead to many concrete conclusions. However, it did allow me to learn all of the aforementioned technical skills and other insights on how to be a contributing member of a productive team. Additionally, I realized that research in the field of cellular biology more closely resembles a marathon than a sprint, and focus on small but consistent positive results generally produces healthy and productive careers, something which I wholeheartedly aim for.


### Current intellectual interests
Many biological problems that appear unrelated share remarkably similar underlying principles. A thymocyte deciding whether to survive negative selection, a cancer cell escaping its primary tumor, an axon growing out of a neuron, a stem cell committing to a lineage and an endothelial cell responding to blood flow all operate within mechanical microenvironments that shape signaling, behavior, and fate. This "mechanobiology thread" that began as an undergraduate is the one I intend on pulling through a PhD. I am interested in how mechanotransduction shapes cell fate, specifically in contexts where it matters clinically as understanding how physical context shapes biological function has potential to benefit more than just fundamental aspects of the field. To give some examples, it has direct implications for understanding autoimmune disease (where thymic selection fails), cancer progression (where the tumor microenvironment drives metastasis), and tissue regeneration (where mechanical cues guide vascular and stem cell behavior). This portfolio is an attempt to build a unified framework for thinking about these problems—one that connects fundamental biology to computational modeling to potential therapeutic interventions.

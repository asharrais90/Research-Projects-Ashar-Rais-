## Introduction to Mechanobiology 
Biological systems are shaped by their molecular composition as well as their physical context. Cells continuously sense mechanical and structural cues that influence signaling, fate decisions, tissue organization, and disease initiation and progression. The term 'mechanobiology' refers to the field of research which studies the role of mechanical forces and physical properties (of cells and surrounding microenvironment) in different hierarchical levels of biological function. In current research environment, these levels range mainly from the molecular level to the level of organs. The foundations of this field were laid in late 19th and early 20th centuries before the advent of the genetics and molecular biology revolution in the mid to late 20th century (Eyckmans et al., 2011). In 1892, Julius Wolff described how trabeculae in bones are aligned with the physical load that is consistently applied on the bone (now referred to as the 'Wolff Law'), suggesting some form of machinery that senses mechanical forces and modulates bone architecture (WOLFF, 1892). Around this time, other researchers like Wilhelm His Sr. and Wilhelm Roux were exploring a different avenue, i.e. the relationship between physical forces and embryogenic morphogenesis (Richardson & Keuck, 2022). In 1917, D'Arcy Thompson published _'On Growth and Form'_ in which he discussed the roles of mechanical forces in formation of diverse body plan in different organisms (Richardson & Keuck, 2022). While these pioneers discussed some fundamental aspects of this field, the technology available at that time was insufficient to allow deeper investigations into questions that were generated by these ideas, slowing down their progress. Then, as the remarkable discoveries in genetics and molecular biology graced the scientific community in mid-20th century, the focus of researchers shifted more towards unraveling questions that were predominantly biochemical in nature.

Fortunately, as time passed further, certain discoveries (for eg., the discovery of mechanosensitive ion channels in 1984 by Guharay and Sachs, (Guharay & Sachs, 1984)) and technological innovations reignited the interest of the community towards answering questions that are more "mechanobiological" in nature. The main developments include the work of Harris, Wild and Stopak (who used synthetic elastic substrates in 1980 to show for the first time that non-muscle cells applied forces on their substrates, a heretical discovery at that time, (Harris et al., 1980)), in-vitro measurement and manipulation of physiological forces (such as replication of strain forces experienced by bones during physical activity and mechanical stretching that mimics pulsating blood flow) and bioreactor-type devices that model chondrocyte compression in different tissues (Eyckmans et al., 2011). The 1990s were particularly productive for the progress of the field of mechanobiology as many important conceptual and technical breakthroughs happened in these years. In 1993, Donald Ingber's tensegrity model was published in Journal of Cell Science and provided the first compelling cell-scale physical framework (Ingber, 1993). Of similar importance were the developments of polyacrylamide gels of tunable stiffness by Pelham & Wang in 1997 (which allowed researchers to manipulate substrate stiffness directly and observe how this affects cellular behavior) (Pelham & Wang, 1997) and Traction Force Microscopy or TFM by Dembo & Wang in 1999 (which opened the path to quantification of actual forces cells apply on their substrates) (Dembo & Wang, 1999).    

These discoveries and breakthroughs at the turn of the millennium paved the way for the current "Molecular Era of Mechanotransduction". Multiple proteins of different kinds (including Integrins, Piezo1&2, Rho GTPase, ROCK, Talin, FAK, LINC complex and YAP/TAZ transcriptional activators) and their associated pathways are now recognized to be involved in connecting mechanical forces to crucial cellular functions (ranging from cellular shape and migration to proliferation and differentiation) (Di et al., 2023). Furthermore, similar to the trend in other biological fields (Qiao et al., 2025), some groups are now incorporating a "systems-approach" to mechanobiological research. This has become apparent from the use of single cell technologies (Kasuba et al., 2026), tools to understand the dynamic and complex nature of tissue architecture (including micro-scale atomic force microscopy, light-sheet elastography, and Brillouin microscopy) (Miranda et al., 2021; Prevedel et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2025) and more advanced materials (such as alginate and hyaluronic acid with dynamic cross-linking) to better recapitulate the mechanical properties of real tissues (Ganesh et al., 2013).

### Significance of exploring mechanobiology
Given the intimate role of mechanical forces and physical properties in cellular functioning and behavior, it is not surprising that decades of mechanobiological research have produced many significant findings in fundamental and translational domains. Diseases ranging from cancer to neurodegenerative disorders have some kind of involvement of mechanobiological processes that can be utilized to better understand and treat these maladies (the term 'mechanomedicine' has been used for collectively referring to this area of research and form of treatment)(Kalukula et al., 2025). Additionally, many groups have been using modern molecular tools to explore the role of mechanical forces and physical properties during developmental processes, particularly those that impart specific form to tissues and organs (Mammoto & Ingber, 2010). This knowledge is further being leveraged in the field of regenerative medicine to improve the treatment of physical injuries and development of artificial limbs and organs (Rayat Pisheh et al., 2026). In the following sections, some interesting findings pertaining to the role of mechanobiology in different diseases, developmental biology and regenerative medicine have been discussed.

#### Diseases and Mechanomedicine
##### Cancer
The involvement and significance of mechanical forces and physical properties in oncogenic transformation is perhaps the most-widely studied and, in the near-term, most consequential frontier of mechanomedicine. A common hallmark of many solid tumors is progressive ECM (extracellular matrix) stiffening and dramatic remodeling, primarily driven by cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) activation, collagen crosslinking, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure (Hanahan, 2022). These processes have been known to promote tumor progression by activating integrin-mediated mechanotransduction pathways (Hamidi & Ivaska, 2018). The formation of pre-metastatic niches (alterations in microenvironment created by a primary tumor in distant organs before cancer cells arrive) also involves certain aspects of mechanical priming prior to tumor cell arrival (Z. Guo et al., 2025). Additionally, the stress-relaxation rate and viscoelastic behavior of the ECM are known to influence the appearance of malignant phenotypes as well by regulating the processes of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and metastatic dissemination (Elosegui-Artola et al., 2023). Tissue curvature and spatial confinement are other important physical properties that influence cancer progression and invasion. Studies on the former have demonstrated that convex curvature promotes a myofibroblast-like phenotype in CAFs and regions with this curvature have been observed to be a preferred spot for cancer stem cells (CSCs) localization (Lee et al., 2016; Nemec et al., 2021). On another front, research work into migrating metastatic clusters have shown that to enable navigation of confined microenvironments, integrin tension is elevated at the trailing edge (Decaris et al., 2021). Spatial confinement (which the cells might encounter more often in a denser, remodeled ECM of solid tumors) also extracts heavy energetic tolls on the cells, driving metabolic reprogramming and migration along the paths of least resistance (Zanotelli et al., 2019). Physical properties of the cells (such as cell and nuclear deformability) themselves play an important role in cancer progression with softer cells exhibiting higher invasive capabilities in confined environments and deformed nuclei being used as biomarkers of metastatic potential in breast cancer (Bell et al., 2022; Kalukula et al., 2025). Interstitial fluid pressure in tumors poses a slightly different problem during treatment, by creating a major barrier to drug delivery that is fundamentally a mechanical problem (Lunt et al., 2008). Following the mention of these points, it is easier to see why targeting the mechanical tumor microenvironment represents a therapeutic axis largely orthogonal to current cytotoxic and immunotherapy approaches, with approaches such as the use of LOX inhibitors, matrix metalloprotease modulation, or CAF reprogramming being explored to improve therapeutic strategies (Boaru et al., 2026; Mustafa et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2023).

##### Fibrosis
Fibrosis (of different types such as pulmonary, hepatic, renal, cardiac and placental) involves pathological ECM deposition and stiffening mainly driven by myofibroblast activation, ECM deposition and aberrant integrin activation (Antar et al., 2023; Faralli et al., 2023). A critical insight from mechanobiology is that this process can be modeled as a self-amplifying feedback loop between transforming growth factor (TGF-β) and force exerted via integrin-Rho-ROCK-YAP signaling (Zhang et al., 2026). TGF-β stored in the ECM in a latent form and is activated and released through mechanical tension applied by certain integrins (Nolte & Margadant, 2020). This further leads to fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition and ECM stiffening, with the later causing the cells to apply more force on the surrounding ECM fibers, exacerbating the fibrotic consequences (Antar et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026). Breaking this loop mechanically (rather than purely biochemically) is an attractive therapeutic target. ROCK inhibitors, integrin antagonists, and stiffness-dissipating matrix-modifying agents are in various stages of investigation in various fibrotic diseases such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and liver fibrosis (Faralli et al., 2023; T. Guo et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022).

##### Mechanoimmunology
The immune system is our primary physiological defense against majority of the diseases and substantial evidence has now accumulated regarding the critical role of mechanical forces in modulating different facets of immunological functioning (C. Zhu, 2026). While immune responses are traditionally considered to be biochemical in nature, studies have shown that they (especially in macrophages and dendritic cells) are indeed affected by the mechanical forces and physical properties of the microenvironment (Chen et al., 2025; Huse, 2017). Immune reactivity of macrophages in response to LPS (lipopolysaccharide) has been observed to be significantly less on soft hydrogel substrates compared to stiffer substrates (McWhorter et al., 2014). In T-cells, the formation of the immunological synapse following ligand binding is crucial for cellular activation as it brings necessary signaling proteins in proximity of each other to initiate and sustain downstream pathways. The mechanical forces applied by the actomyosin network are central to the formation of IS as they drive the clustering of TCR (T-cell receptor) and integrins towards the center of contact (Chao et al., 2025). Therefore, it is not surprising that disruption of actomyosin functioning through different approaches hamper IS formation and consequently, T-cell activation (Kumari et al., 2014).       

##### Vascular Pathologies
Cells that make up the vascular system are frequently subjected to different types of mechanical forces, most prominently the shear forces produced by flowing fluid (blood and lymph) in the circulatory vessels. Variations in these forces have been observed to significantly modulate the behavior of endothelial cells (ECs, which line the interior of blood vessels) (Davies, 2009). In regions where shear forces are non-uniform or abnormally low levels (such as arterial branches and bifurcations), aberrant activation of signaling pathways has been observed to produce inflammation, increase vessel permeability and atherosclerotic plaque formation(Tamargo et al., 2023). Other pathologies such as hypertension, arrhythmias and preeclampsia are also associated with dysregulation of mechanical homeostasis (Kalukula et al., 2025). The mechanobiological perspective is also being utilized to develop novel research, diagnostic and therapeutic tools. Microstretcher platforms that mimic the hypertensive stress upon ECs have shown how these cells adapt to these pathological forces, providing insights for work into endocytosis-based drug delivery (Dessalles et al., 2021; Leclech et al., 2022). Additionally, endothelial shear stress measurements are now being incorporated with coronary computed tomography (CT) angiography to develop non-invasive mapping of shear stress for improving myocardial perfusion abnormalities prediction and aiding in risk stratification (Hong et al., 2023). On a different frontier, clinical trials are ongoing to explore how targeting of mechanosensitive pathways such as p38 MAPK can help in the treatment of lamin-related cardiomyopathies (Bonne & Muchir, 2017; Miroshnikova et al., 2019), offering another avenue to leverage mechanobiological knowledge to advance cardiovascular therapies.

##### Aging and Neurodegenerative Disorders
As tissues age, they are observed to stiffen mainly due to excessive collagen crosslinking, loss of elastin and increased collagen density (Panwar et al., 2015). This mechanical shift has been demonstrated to alter the behavior of resident stem cells in different tissues, ranging from hematopoietic stem cell niche in the bone marrow to the niches of muscle satellite cells (Brunet et al., 2023). These aging tissues all show altered mechanical properties which has been correlated with diminished regenerative capacity and decrease in health outcomes. The brain is especially vulnerable to mechanical stress due to its rheological properties and limited adaptive capacity and it has been observed that the brain cannot adapt to short-term mechanical changes at the level of other tissues (Gupta & Sen, 2016). This is important because resident cells (including neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and microglia) are mechanosensitive and abnormal mechanical stress has been demonstrated to inhibit axonal regrowth and modulate inflammatory signaling pathways (Kalukula et al., 2025), both of which are considered to be common factors in the development of numerous neurodegenerative disorders (Salvadores et al., 2017; W. Zhang et al., 2023). Replication of mechanical forces following traumatic brain injury in in-vitro systems have demonstrated induction of glial inflammatory states due to single or uniaxial and biaxial deformations (Kalukula et al., 2025). Furthermore, other studies that introduced mechanically activated microglial cells into healthy cortical neuronal networks in microfluidic devices reported synaptic pruning activity independently of neuronal injury (Krukowski et al., 2021), shedding some light on the long-term effects of mechanical forces upon neuronal health.
 

#### Developmental Biology and Regenerative Medicine
A remarkable amount of work has been done to understand the plethora of chemical cues and genes that regulate the process of morphogenesis (assembly of cells into complex tissues and organs). However, these molecular signals are not sufficient alone to completely explain the complexity of morphogenetic processes and different mechanobiological concepts have been brought into the picture to fill the gaps. Studies have reported the importance of ECM identity and spatial composition in guiding local and global tissue geometry (Rozario & DeSimone, 2010). The resident cells themselves remodel their physical environment by changing the composition, alignment and cross-linking of the matrix proteins forming a bidirectional relationship that changes temporally to produce the dynamic architecture observed in tissues (Clause & Barker, 2013). In addition to the viscoelasticity, other physical properties in developing tissues/organs which are observed to change over time include tension, shear, hydrostatic pressure and anisotropy and these state transitions are now considered an important component of morphogenesis (Ben Amar & Jia, 2013; Heisenberg & Bellaïche, 2013). Development of the complex architecture of the lungs is one of the best examples to grasp the role of mechanobiology in formation of tissue/organ structure. In the embryonic phase, the airways are filled with fluid and the pressure of the luminal fluid is normally higher than that in the surrounding pleural cavity, generating a positive transmural (pressure across the developing lung (Hooper et al., 2015; Jaslove et al., 2022). Experiments using embryonic lung tissue of different origins have shown that changing this pressure can alter branching morphogenesis of the epithelial tube which is the progenitor of the highly branched airway tree and alveolar structure. In these studies, higher transmural pressure has both promoted and suppressed specific types of branching and produced gene-expression profiles more similar to developing lungs in vivo (Mohr-Allen et al., 2025; Nelson et al., 2017; Unbekandt et al., 2008).

As our understanding of mechanobiology has evolved over time, the principles in this field have been applied towards the advancement of regenerative medicine (whose main focus is on repairing, replacing or regenerating human tissues and organs to replace normal physiological functioning) (Sampogna et al., 2015). Stable, biomimetic scaffolds that replicate central features of the ECM have been employed in many different contexts to support essential cellular functions and help in treatment of cancer, burn wounds, osteoarthritis (Gao et al., 2023; Martinet et al., 2025) and organ regeneration for heart muscle recovery, spinal cord repair and bioartificial organ development (Cai et al., 2023; Karageorgos et al., 2024; Rayat Pisheh et al., 2026). Engineered viscoelastic collagen-I hydrogels have been explored in modulation of cytotoxic activity of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells which are now regularly used in therapeutic strategies of many cancers(Adu-Berchie et al., 2023). Furthermore, synthetic viscoelastic antigen-presenting cells (SynVACs) which are composed of anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 functionalized alginate-based hydrogels have been reported to promote CAR T-cell expansion, increase antitumor activity and improve survival of these immune cells in-vivo (Z. Liu et al., 2024). One of the main roles of mechanobiology in regenerative medicine is providing more thorough methods of regulating stem cell activity, as the fate and function of these cells have been reported to be highly responsive to mechanical cues (Hu et al., 2023). Softer substrates (with mechanical properties resembling that of brain tissue) have been observed to promote specific chromatin organizational states which favor neural lineage commitment. Conversely, stiffer substrates (which mimic bone tissue's mechanical properties) induce chromatin condensation and gene expression profiles associated with osteogenic differentiation (Kalukula et al., 2025). Another promising set of applications of mechanobiology and hydrogels comes in the treatment of injured tissues. Synthetic hydrogels have been loaded with stem cells of specific types to promote healing in these damage regions, and in addition to providing specific physical cues for stem cell differentiation, functionalities such as scavenging of ROS (reactive oxygen species) have also been added to the hydrogels to deal with the hostile components of the injured microenvironment and increase healing (D. Liu et al., 2023).   

The vast potential and diverse benefits of mechanobiology make it an exciting field to pursue, with future findings likely to unravel significant mysteries in biological research. Within this broad field, my attention is drawn to a specific set of questions: how mechanical forces and physical microenvironmental properties regulate cellular behavior, tissue organization, and disease initiation and progression, and how diverse tools such as computational biology and nanotechnology can be used to study or manipulate these systems. The projects that comprise the upcoming portfolio grow out of this focus.

## Portfolio

#### [[Project 1- Thymic selection mechanobiology]]
Thymic selection is a central part of T-cell development which occurs in the thymus (a gland/organ situated above the heart). This process is crucial for the building of a self-tolerant (does not attack self antigens) and competent (can adequately attack foreign antigens) T-cell population in our body. Although a substantial amount of work has been undertaken to understand thymic selection, there are still many open questions that demand more research or a different perspective of doing research. The project in this portfolio arises from the latter method and focuses on the mechanobiology of thymic selection. This project is built upon the central question of how the mechanical properties of thymic stroma (such as stiffness, fiber orientation and viscoelasticity) influence thymic selection process. It is composed of two sub-projects:
1. A scoping review that explores the literature on the connection between mechanical properties and thymic selection process to delineate what has been studied and what gaps remain exist that might be explored in the future;
2. A simulation model built on the principles of agent-based modeling and ordinary differential equations to study how stromal stiffness would affect the ratio of selection outcomes.
